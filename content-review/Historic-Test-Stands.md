### Current Status: PRESERVED - National Historic Landmark (1986)

**Stop ID:** stop10

**Tour Time:** ~15 min

### Current Content

**Title:** Redstone Test Stand: Where It Starts  
**Short Title:** Redstone Test Stand  
**Subtitle:** National Historic Landmark · Dodd Road  
**Location:** Dodd Road, MSFC / Redstone Arsenal  

### Hook
"Every test stand on this tour traces back to this one."

### Full Narration (~44 seconds)
"Every test stand on this tour traces back to this one. In the early 1950s, Wernher von Braun's Army Ballistic Missile Agency team static-fired Redstone missiles right here — the oldest test stand still standing on Marshall's campus. That same Redstone design, modified into the Mercury-Redstone launch vehicle, went on to carry Alan Shepard on America's first human spaceflight in 1961. The stand earned National Historic Landmark status in 1986. Twelve years and one moon landing later, the lessons learned firing a single Redstone engine here helped build the stands that would hold down a five-engine Saturn Five."

### Why It Matters
Every rocket test program at Marshall has roots here. Before Saturn, before the Space Shuttle, this stand proved out the Redstone: the missile-turned-launch-vehicle that gave America its first astronaut in space. The static-fire test method itself, proven here first, shaped every stand built afterward.

### Key Facts
- **1950s Redstone test campaign** - Army Ballistic Missile Agency era, before NASA existed
- **1961 Alan Shepard's flight** - A modified Redstone launched America's first astronaut
- **1985 National Historic Landmark** - Designated by the U.S. Department of the Interior

### Deep Dive: From Missile to Mercury-Redstone
Assembled and tested at what became Marshall Space Flight Center, the Mercury-Redstone launch vehicle was derived directly from the Army's Redstone missile, itself proven on this stand. Engineers here installed a Mercury capsule and its escape system atop the booster and test-fired the assembly before it was ever trusted to carry a human being. The stand is documented by the Library of Congress's Historic American Engineering Record (HAER AL-129-A).

With almost all of the stand's $25,000 construction budget sunk into its concrete foundation, there was no money left to build a proper instrumentation bunker — so Test Lab engineers cleaned out two decommissioned railroad tank cars that had once hauled chemicals at the arsenal during World War II, packed them with recording and control equipment, and buried them under a mound of earth about 100 yards from the stand (HAER AL-129-A).

## STOP 5: Neutral Buoyancy Simulator (Building 4705) [DEMOLISHED DEC 2025]

**Stop ID:** stop5

**Branch:** ET20 Experimental Fluids & Environmental  
**Tour Time:** ~15 min  
**Status:** Legacy site, Available (as historic marker)  
**Accessibility:** Yes  

### Current Content

**Title:** Neutral Buoyancy Simulator: Rehearsing Weightlessness  
**Short Title:** Neutral Buoyancy Simulator  
**Subtitle:** Legacy site · Building 4705, 1968–1997  
**Location:** Former Building 4705 (demolished, Dec 2025)  

### Hook
"You're standing where NASA first learned to fake weightlessness at scale."

### Full Narration (~46 seconds)
"You're standing where NASA first learned to fake weightlessness at scale. From 1968 to 1997, a tank seventy-five feet across and forty feet deep — nearly a million and a half gallons of water — let astronauts train underwater for the true weightlessness of orbit. When Skylab launched damaged in 1973, the fix was rehearsed here first. When astronauts serviced the Hubble Space Telescope, they practiced the repair here first. This facility closed in 1997 when a larger lab opened in Houston, and its tank was demolished in December 2025 — but the training method it proved out is still how NASA prepares for every spacewalk today."

### Why It Matters
Weightlessness can't be faked on the ground — except underwater, where buoyancy can be tuned to cancel out gravity. That let astronauts rehearse real repair tasks, with real tools, for hours at a time, long before ever reaching orbit. This tank proved the method that saved a space station and kept the Hubble Space Telescope working for decades.

### Key Facts
- **1.5M gallons** - 75 ft across, 40 ft deep — NASA's first large-scale weightlessness trainer
- **1968–97 years in operation** - Closed when JSC's larger Neutral Buoyancy Laboratory opened
- **2025 demolition began** - The tank came down in December 2025

### Deep Dive: Saving Skylab and Servicing Hubble
When Skylab's meteoroid shield tore away during launch in 1973, engineers rehearsed the emergency repair procedures underwater here before the crew ever attempted them in orbit — helping save the station. Decades later, astronauts including Kathryn Thornton and Jeffrey Hoffman trained here for Hubble Space Telescope servicing, and the facility supported early International Space Station and Japanese Experimental Module mockup testing before closing in 1997.

Before any tank existed, the whole concept was proven after-hours in 1965, when MSFC engineer Charles Cooper and colleague Charles Stocks flooded a hollow, plugged gyroscope mockup inside an out-of-service metal-quenching tank until it hung neutrally buoyant — the improvised test that convinced Marshall managers to fund a full-scale facility. In recognition of the role that facility went on to play across Skylab, Space Shuttle, and Hubble missions, it was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1985 (HAER AL-129-B).

Thirty years of spacewalk training happened inside that cutaway before the facility closed in 1997 and was demolished in late 2025."

## STOP 6: Dynamic Test Stand (Building 4550) [DEMOLISHED JAN 2026]

### Current Status: DEMOLISHED - Legacy Site

**Stop ID:** stop6

**Branch:** ET40 Structural Dynamics  
**Tour Time:** ~15 min  
**Status:** Legacy site, Available  
**Accessibility:** Yes  

### Current Content

**Title:** Dynamic Test Stand: Shaking the Whole Stack  
**Short Title:** Dynamic Test Stand  
**Subtitle:** Legacy site · West Test Area, Building 4550  
**Location:** Former Building 4550 (demolished, Jan 2026)  

### Hook
"A rocket doesn't just fly straight up — it flexes, bends, and vibrates as it goes. Getting those vibrations wrong can shake a vehicle apart."

### Full Narration (~46 seconds)
"A rocket doesn't just fly straight up — it flexes, bends, and vibrates as it goes. Getting those vibrations wrong can shake a vehicle apart. Right here, in 1978, engineers assembled the Space Shuttle Orbiter Enterprise, its external tank, and its twin solid rocket boosters into full liftoff configuration — the first time the entire Shuttle stack had ever been mated vertically — and then deliberately shook the whole thing. The Mated Vertical Ground Vibration Test measured exactly how the stack would flex in flight, long before any Shuttle ever left the ground. This stand stood until January 2026, when it was brought down along with the nearby T-Tower."

### Why It Matters
A launch vehicle isn't rigid — it bends and resonates as engines fire and aerodynamic forces build. If a vehicle's structure resonates at the wrong frequency, the result can be catastrophic. Before the Space Shuttle ever flew, its complete stack was assembled here and shaken on purpose, so engineers could measure its true vibration modes and make sure the real thing would fly safely.

### Key Facts
- **360 feet tall** - Topped by a 64-ft derrick — once the tallest man-made structure in North Alabama
- **1978 first full-stack test** - First time orbiter, tank, and boosters were mated vertically
- **MVGVT the test's name** - Mated Vertical Ground Vibration Test — measuring how the stack would flex in flight
- **2026 demolished** - Imploded January 10, 2026, alongside the nearby T-Tower

### Deep Dive: Why Shake a Whole Rocket
Built in 1964, this 360-foot tower was originally built to test the Saturn V: fully assembled Saturn V vehicles were suspended inside it and shaken to find their bending modes, the same low-frequency flex patterns that dictated how the real rocket's guidance system would have to respond in flight.

The stand later hosted the Space Shuttle's Mated Vertical Ground Vibration Test in 1978 — the first time an orbiter, external tank, and both solid rocket boosters were stacked together vertically anywhere. Even after Shuttle testing wound down, the stand kept working: it was last used in the early 2000s for microgravity research before standing dormant for two more decades, until its implosion in January 2026.

Built into one bay along the stand's east side starting in 1968, a lesser-known feature called the Zero Gravity Drop Tower let a shielded capsule fall 294 feet down vertical guide rails while air thrusters pushed it faster than ordinary free-fall, letting test packages inside float free of the capsule floor for as long as 4.3 seconds before the capsule slammed into a catch tube, where a trapped-air cushion and 5 feet of rubberized horsehair matting brought it to a stop (HAER AL-129-C).

- Redstone: ABMA → NASA (1960)

**4. Preservation Decisions**
- Why preserve Redstone but not T-Tower?

**5. Legacy Lives On**
- Redstone: Test methodology still used
- NBS: Training method continued at JSC
- Dynamic Stand: Vibration testing principles unchanged
