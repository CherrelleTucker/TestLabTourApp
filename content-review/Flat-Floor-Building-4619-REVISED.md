# SECTION 2: SCIENCE (How It Works)

## Official Capability Description (from Power Apps)

This capability provides **Guidance, Navigation, and Control (GN&C) testing** using a precision air-bearing epoxy floor (44 x 86 feet, the largest of its kind in the world) for simulating near-zero gravity in three axes. A robotic arm is combined with a gantry to provide 8 degrees of freedom for simulating relative motion with respect to a fixed target. In addition, a dynamic lighting simulator can simulate the motion and brightness of the sun. The Contact Dynamics Simulation Lab tests how a spacecraft handles the contact of docking or capture.

## Current "Deep Dive" Content

**Title:** Go deeper: how it works

**Content:**
A dynamic lighting simulator reproduces the motion and brightness of the sun — including the harsh, low-angle lighting of the Moon's South Pole — so Artemis III crews and hardware can train for conditions that can otherwise hide the ground itself. The same floor doubles as a test bed for a very different kind of present-day work: small, tethered "space tug" simulators that fly across the epoxy on air bearings to rehearse in-space servicing and assembly. In one recent demonstration [SME INPUT NEEDED] [NEED YEAR], a tug simulator flew up to a floating weld platform, where two aluminum parts were clamped together and joined by a laser welder mounted on a robotic arm — a rehearsal for repairing and building hardware in orbit instead of only on the ground.

Long before Artemis, this room went by a different name: the Flight Robotics Laboratory. Through the 1980s and '90s, an overhead 8-degree-of-freedom manipulator — with a working volume of 160 by 40 by 23 feet — flew simulated targets across the floor so engineers could test automatic rendezvous-and-capture algorithms for Space Shuttle and Space Station docking mechanisms, years before any of that hardware flew.

The epoxy floor itself dates to the late 1960s [SME INPUT NEEDED] [NEED SPECIFIC YEAR] and has been re-poured and re-leveled several times since — the current surface is its fourth pour, precise enough that a test article the size of a small car can be nudged into motion by a light push and glide for minutes without noticeably slowing. Self-leveling epoxy floors like this one are genuinely hard to get flat enough for the job — even small dips or high spots are enough to send a floating test article drifting off course. Overhead, the DOTS gantry — Dynamic Overhead Target System — carries sensor payloads across the floor to simulate a target vehicle's approach from above, independent of whatever is floating below.
